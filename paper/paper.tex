\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation pipelines trust whatever a retriever returns, so one
poisoned document can steer an answer. Cheap detectors exist, but their accuracy is
usually reported on the corpus they were tuned on. We compare eight document-level
methods, built from pattern matching, embedding distance, perplexity, a sentence
classifier and a published classifier, under one protocol: group-aware splits,
thresholds set on training documents for a fixed false-alarm budget, and a held-out
role scored once. A fusion of the sentence classifier with the original signals
catches most attacks at a five percent budget, but the gain is uneven: the original
signals flag fluent passages without instructions no more often than they flag clean
ones, and at a one percent budget the fusion keeps most instruction-style attacks but
catches almost none of those passages. A control that sees only character n-grams
matches the classifier on those passages, so its recall there reflects corpus style,
not instruction detection. When a whole source is held out, the pooled threshold
flags nearly every clean document or almost no attacks. Because attacks are rare,
the precision of an alert falls quickly with prevalence. Perplexity dominates
latency; a cascade that stops early on clearly clean documents saves more than half
of that time on clean traffic, at a recall cost that is small at a one percent budget
and larger at five percent.
\end{abstract}
