\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation systems answer a query by retrieving documents
from a corpus and conditioning a language model's response on their contents
\cite{lewis2020rag}. The architecture is now standard in production assistants, but it inherits a
quiet assumption: that every document in the corpus is trustworthy. When that
assumption fails the result is \textit{knowledge poisoning}, the deliberate
insertion of a document designed to manipulate downstream generation. The concern
is not hypothetical: indirect prompt injection through externally sourced content
has been demonstrated against production LLM-integrated applications
\cite{greshake2023indirect,yi2023benchmarking}, and attacks that inject a single malicious passage per
targeted query have been shown to be practical at scale \cite{zhang2025practical}.

A poison detection system (PDS) sits between the retriever and the generator and
screens each document before it is trusted. The signals available for this
task are cheap and well known: instruction-pattern matching, distance from a
trusted corpus in embedding space, language-model fluency, and small learned text
classifiers. Papers usually report them on the corpus they were tuned on. Our
question is different: when the signals are combined and tested on documents they
did not see, \emph{where do they fail, and what does it cost to fix them?}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{One protocol.} A pooled set of 2,291 documents from two public
    benchmarks and in-house data, group-aware splits for three seeds, thresholds set
    on training clean documents for a fixed false-alarm budget, and a held-out
    role scored once (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
  \item \textbf{A held-out comparison} of eight methods at 5\% and 1\% budgets,
    with group-bootstrap intervals and the precision an alert has when attacks are
    rare (Sections~\ref{sec:heldout} and~\ref{sec:precision}).
  \item \textbf{A failure analysis.} Recall by source and attack type; a
    style-only control showing that part of the strongest signal reads corpus style;
    a leave-one-source-out test in which the pooled threshold fails; and a
    development-set pipeline that scored well on 77 documents and fell to chance on
    new sources (Sections~\ref{sec:fail}--\ref{sec:learned}).
  \item \textbf{A cost analysis.} Per-signal latency on a laptop CPU and two
    cost-aware cascades, with what they save and what they lose
    (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
  \item \textbf{Development-set case studies}, reported as such: a score-scale
    mismatch that cost an equal-weight average much of its accuracy, an evasion we
    call boilerplate anchoring, and negative results (Section~\ref{sec:devset}).
\end{enumerate}
