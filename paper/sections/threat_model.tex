\section{Threat Model and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:threat-model}

\subsection{System Model}
We consider a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline with a document corpus
$C$, a retriever $R$ and a downstream language model $M$. At query time $R$ selects
documents from $C$ and passes them to $M$ as context. PDS sits between $R$ and $M$
(or runs offline over $C$): each candidate document $d$ is scored before it is
allowed to influence $M$.

\subsection{Attacker Capabilities}
We assume an attacker who can introduce or modify at least one document that is
later retrieved, for example by contributing to a corpus with open ingestion (a
wiki, a shared documentation repository, a user-submitted knowledge base) or by
having a page indexed by a web-scraped source; poisoning web-scale sources has
been shown to be practical~\cite{carlini2024poisoning}. Prior work shows that a single
injected passage per targeted query can be enough~\cite{zhang2025practical}, so
we do not assume control of a large share of the corpus. The attacker's goal is
to make $M$ follow an injected instruction, leak information or give an
attacker-chosen answer.

The attacker cannot modify the retriever or the weights, prompts or system
instructions of $M$, cannot guarantee retrieval, and cannot observe PDS scores or
thresholds when building an attack. Our in-house adversarial documents assume
the attacker knows the general approach (pattern, embedding and perplexity
scoring) but not the parameters.

\subsection{Attack Types}
The pooled set (Table~\ref{tab:pool}) contains three kinds of attack.
\emph{Inserted instructions} (BIPIA): an explicit instruction placed at the start,
middle or end of an ordinary email, table or code document.
\emph{Steering passages} (PoisonedRAG): fluent paragraphs that assert an
attacker-chosen answer to one question and contain no instruction at all.
\emph{In-house documents}, in four categories: instruction injection
(``ignore previous instructions,'' fake system tags), authority spoofing (a fake
policy or administrator notice), corpus poisoning (harmful advice in ordinary
technical language) and perplexity artefacts (unnaturally smooth or garbled
text). The red-teaming set adds a fifth, found during evaluation: credential
exposure phrased as neutral boilerplate (Section~\ref{sec:devset}).

\subsection{Defender Assumptions}
The defender sees the full text of a document before trusting it and has
(i) a small set of known-clean documents for the embedding reference, and
(ii) labelled clean and poisoned documents from the same sources to fit the
fusion and the sentence classifier and to set thresholds. The second assumption
is stronger than in earlier versions of this work, and Section~\ref{sec:loso}
shows what happens when it fails for a new source. The defender also has a latency
budget, which motivates the cascade of Section~\ref{sec:system}.

\subsection{Formal Problem Statement}
Let $y(d)\in\{0,1\}$ mark a poisoned document and let the detector flag $d$ when
its score $s(d)$ exceeds a threshold $\tau$. For a false-alarm budget $\alpha$ the
defender wants the most sensitive threshold that respects the budget,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
    \max_{\tau}\ \Pr\big[s(d)>\tau\mid y=1\big]\\
    \text{s.t.}\ \Pr\big[s(d)>\tau\mid y=0\big]\le\alpha .
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}
Both probabilities are estimated from finite samples and the threshold is fixed
before the held-out documents are seen, so the realised false-alarm rate on them can differ
from $\alpha$ (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Each signal $i$ also has a cost $c_i$,
and a policy $\pi$ chooses which signals run on a document. The cost-aware problem
is to minimise the expected cost under the targets of Eq.~\eqref{eq:problem},
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{d}\Big[\sum_{i\in\pi(d)}c_i\Big],
    \label{eq:costproblem}
\end{equation}
where Section~\ref{sec:system} gives the two cascades we measure as heuristic
solutions, not optimal ones.

\subsection{Out of Scope}
We do not study attacks on the retriever's ranking, attacks on $M$ after a
document has been allowed through, colluding multi-document attacks in which no
single document is suspicious, or adaptive attackers with white-box access to the
detectors and thresholds.
