\section{Limitations and Negative Results}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Dataset Scale and Composition}
The main evaluation scores 77 documents (17 held-out clean and 60
poisoned across 4 categories), plus 10 adversarial and 12 cross-domain
documents. A meaningful share of the poisoned and adversarial documents
were authored by the team rather than drawn from independent real-world
sources, and the perplexity detector uses base GPT-2. Results at this scale
should be read as a methodology demonstration, not as evidence that the
detectors generalize broadly.
\emph{Addressed by:} Phase 2, which scales the dataset to 300+ documents
drawn from public sources (BIPIA, PoisonedRAG, deepset/prompt-injections,
Open-Prompt-Injection, HackAPrompt) with licenses and provenance logged
per source.

\subsection{Development-Set Tuning}
Detector thresholds, calibration bounds and the fusion threshold were all
chosen on the same 77 documents that the reported F1 values are computed
on, and some choices (the generalized ``ignore \ldots\ instructions''
pattern and the credential patterns) were made after inspecting individual
failures in that set. All fusion F1 values are therefore development-set
results and are optimistic.
\emph{Addressed by:} Phase 1, a fixed train/validation/test split in which
thresholds are chosen on validation data only and the test split is
evaluated once on a frozen code version, with bootstrap confidence
intervals.

\subsection{No Comparison Against Published Baselines}
All F1 numbers compare fusion variants of PDS's own three detectors against
each other. There is no comparison against published prompt-injection or
poisoning detectors (e.g., a DeBERTa-based classifier, Meta's Prompt Guard),
so we cannot claim PDS matches the state of the art; we can only claim that,
among our own fusion strategies, calibration gives a large, measurable
improvement.
\emph{Addressed by:} Phase 3 (baseline selection, wrapping, and a
protocol-compliant comparison table).

\subsection{Domain Dependence of the Embedding Detector}
On the 12 benign cross-domain documents (HR and support policies), the
calibrated fusion flagged all 12 at the in-domain threshold, every flag
driven by the embedding detector (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The embedding
baseline is built only from software READMEs, so the detector partly
measures distance from that domain rather than malicious intent. The
in-domain F1 must therefore not be read as domain-general, and part of the
embedding detector's success on the corpus-poisoning category may be topic
distance.
\emph{Addressed by:} a domain-diverse embedding baseline (held-out
evaluation on cross-domain documents) and the larger multi-domain dataset
of Phase 2.

\subsection{Negative Results: Alternative Fusion and Segment-Level Scoring}
We also implemented three variants motivated by the boilerplate-anchoring
finding: segment-level (per-sentence) embedding scoring with a maximum
aggregate, a blend of maximum and mean segment scores, and a veto rule
(any single segment above a high absolute threshold forces a flag).
Max-over-segments raised false positives on the 17 held-out clean documents
from 4 to 9 at the original threshold; after re-tuning, peak F1 was 0.90
(maximum), 0.90 (blend) and 0.92 (veto), none above the 0.92 of the
unmodified document-level embedding detector. The adversarial document
\texttt{adv\_010} remained hard: its highest segment score (0.319) was below
the maximum segment score of 5 of the 17 clean documents, so no threshold
separated it from clean text without added false positives. We report this
as a negative result: sentence-level scores were noisier than the
document-level average, and the dominant accuracy lever was fixing the
score-scale mismatch rather than restructuring how scores are aggregated.

\subsection{Calibration Fragility}
Section~\ref{sec:calibration-fragility} shows that min-max calibration
bounds for the embedding detector are set by a single document at each
extreme: removing just that document shifts the whole dataset's normalized
scores by an average of 2.1\%, roughly 1.8x more than under quantile
(5th/95th percentile) bounds. Quantile calibration being more
\emph{stable} does not automatically make it more \emph{effective}: under
quantile bounds, \texttt{adv\_010} actually receives a lower embedding
contribution (0.215 versus 0.318). Switching calibration methods would
require re-tuning the decision threshold alongside it.
\emph{Addressed by:} Phase 5's conformal calibration work, which chooses
thresholds with a formal false-positive-rate guarantee.

\subsection{Adversarial Evaluation Is Narrow and Partly Patched}
The first red-teaming round caught 9 of 10 documents; the evasion
(\texttt{adv\_010}) was credential-exposure content framed as generic
license text. We then added two credential-exposure patterns written after
reading that document's wording, after which all 10 are caught. This is a
fix for a gap that red-teaming found, not independent evidence of
robustness: the same team wrote the attacks and the patches, and none of
the attacks used obfuscation (homoglyphs, zero-width characters, base64,
splitting across documents). The 10/10 figure must not be read as an
estimate of robustness against an adaptive, obfuscation-aware attacker.
\emph{Addressed by:} Phase 8 (fresh detector-aware and obfuscated attacks,
paraphrase robustness, multi-document and position-sensitivity testing).

\subsection{Latency Measurement Methodology}
The reported 70.5\% latency reduction of the staged pipeline
(\texttt{eval/staged\_pipeline.py}) is computed by replaying per-detector
latencies logged during a single evaluation run on one machine. It is not a
systematic benchmark: there is no repeated-run median or p95, and an
earlier run of the same pipeline gave 72.5\%, which illustrates the
run-to-run variation. The ordering (pattern $\ll$ embedding $\ll$
perplexity) is robust, but the exact percentage should be treated as
indicative.
\emph{Addressed by:} Phase 1's latency benchmark
(\texttt{eval/latency\_benchmark.py}), with warm-up isolation and
$\geq$100 runs per detector.

\subsection{Calibration Staleness Over Time}
Calibration bounds are computed once from the development set and reused
for every later document (the adversarial and cross-domain scripts freeze
them deliberately to avoid circularity). Whether they go stale as the
incoming document distribution shifts is untested.
\emph{Addressed by:} not yet scheduled; an open question for future work.
