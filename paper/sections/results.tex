\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

All numbers in Sections~\ref{sec:heldout}--\ref{sec:cost} come from the held-out
test role of the three splits (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}): $\resNPosA$ poisoned
and $\resNNegA$ clean documents for seed $\resSeedA$, and the same sizes for the
other two. Recall, false-alarm rate and precision are means over the three seeds,
with the range over seeds where it matters. The seeds overlap
heavily, so this range shows stability to the split, not statistical
significance; the 95\% intervals in the tables are group-bootstrap intervals
within a seed. Sections~\ref{sec:transfer}, \ref{sec:loso} and~\ref{sec:learned} are diagnostics on
development, training and validation documents, and say so; the test role is not used there.

\subsection{Held-out detection at fixed false-alarm budgets}
\label{sec:heldout}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} and Fig.~\ref{fig:roc} compare all methods at the 5\%
and 1\% budgets. Test recall matches validation recall for the fused model (0.68
at the 5\% budget on both) but is lower for the sentence classifier alone (0.59
against 0.64) and for the published classifier (0.07 against 0.11).

At the 5\% budget the fused LR\,+\,sentence classifier flags
$\resTPRFiveLRsen$ of poisoned documents (95\% interval
$\resTPRloFiveLRsen$--$\resTPRhiFiveLRsen$; $\resTPRminFiveLRsen$--$\resTPRmaxFiveLRsen$

\subsection{Where the detectors fail}
\label{sec:fail}
Recall is not uniform across sources and attack types (Fig.~\ref{fig:source},
Table~\ref{tab:source}, Fig.~\ref{fig:attack}). The sources differ in what an
attack looks like. BIPIA documents contain an inserted instruction, which the
pattern bank and the sentence classifier can pick up; PoisonedRAG passages are
fluent paragraphs written to steer an answer and usually contain no trigger
phrase, so wording-based signals have little to hold on to. At the 5\% budget
the pattern bank and perplexity flag none of them, and the embedding signal and
the logistic regression on the three original signals flag them about as often as
they flag clean passages of the same source (recall 0.16 against a false-alarm
rate of 0.15, and 0.23 against 0.22). The sentence classifier flags 0.40 of them
and the fused model 0.61, but at the 1\% budget these fall to 0.03 and 0.09,
while the fused model's recall on BIPIA only moves from 0.75 to 0.68.
Section~\ref{sec:learned} asks why.
% per-source numbers above: tab_source / final_test_seed*.md, checked by paper/check_claims.py --final
The false-alarm
panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:source} shows the second failure: with one pooled

The in-house source is small

\subsection{What an alert costs}
\label{sec:precision}
Recall and false-alarm rate say little about how useful an alert is when attacks
are rare. By Eq.~\ref{eq:precision}, at 1\% poisoned documents the fused model
at the 5\% budget has a precision of $\resPrecFiveLRsenPrevOne$, and at 0.1\% only
$\resPrecFiveLRsenPrevTenth$; the 1\% budget lifts these to
$\resPrecOneLRsenPrevOne$ and $\resPrecOneLRsenPrevTenth$ at the price of recall.
The published classifier reaches $\resPrecFivePubPrevOne$ at 1\% poisoned
documents. Fig.~\ref{fig:precision} and Table~\ref{tab:precision} show the whole
range. In other words, a detector that flags a large share of attacks can still
bury a reviewer in false alarms when poisoned documents are rare.

\subsection{Why the 1\% budget is hard}
Fig.~\ref{fig:scores} shows the fused score of clean and poisoned documents for
one seed, with the thresholds of both budgets. Most of the recall that is lost
when the budget tightens comes from one source: between the 5\% and the 1\%
budget the fused model's recall on PoisonedRAG falls from 0.61 to 0.09, while on
BIPIA it falls only from 0.75 to 0.68. At the 1\% budget about nine in ten
PoisonedRAG passages score below the threshold, that is, inside the range of the
clean documents, so a threshold that tolerates fewer false alarms rejects them
first. These are the passages that wording-based signals do not see, which
suggests that adding more signals of the same kind would not help.

\subsection{Latency and the cost of the cascade}
\label{sec:cost}
Table~\ref{tab:latency} and Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} give the cost of each signal
on one laptop CPU. Cost does not track usefulness: the sentence classifier,
the strongest single signal, is nearly free (median $\resLatMedSen$\,ms) and the
pattern bank is cheaper still ($\resLatMedPat$\,ms), while perplexity is the most
expensive of our own signals (median $\resLatMedPpl$\,ms, 95th percentile
$\resLatPNinetyFivePpl$\,ms) and the weakest at the 5\% budget.
Latency grows with document length
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}b), which explains the long tail of the 95th percentile.

\subsection{The development-set pipeline does not transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}
Before building the pooled protocol we applied the frozen 77-document pipeline,
unchanged, to 60 clean and 60 poisoned documents from each of two public sources
(BIPIA and PoisonedRAG) that played no part in its development; nothing was tuned
on them. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} shows the result. At the development threshold
of 0.15 the fusion flags 98\% (BIPIA) and 100\% (PoisonedRAG) of the poisoned
documents, and also essentially every clean document (false-alarm rate 1.00), so
its precision is 0.50, which is what flagging everything gives. The
threshold-free AUC of every signal and of the fusion lies between 0.44 and 0.64,
close to a coin flip. The development F1 of 0.97 therefore describes how well
thresholds fit 77 documents, not a detector that carries over.

\subsection{A new source: leave-one-source-out}
\label{sec:loso}

Table~\ref{tab:headline} measures detection on new documents from known
sources. A deployer who meets a new source is in a harder position. We simulated
it on the training and validation roles only: train on two source groups, set the
threshold for the 5\% budget from the clean documents of those groups, and apply
the model and the threshold unchanged to the third group (Table~\ref{tab:loso}).

The threshold does not carry over, and the ranking does not always either. On
BIPIA the sentence classifier flags 0.85--0.89 of the poisoned documents and also
0.76--0.80 of the clean ones, with an AUC of only 0.58--0.60: its recall comes
from flagging almost everything. On PoisonedRAG the sentence classifier and the
fused model flag 1\% of the poisoned documents and no clean ones, although their
AUC is 0.69--0.71; the ranking holds some signal, but the threshold set on the
other sources sits above it. The pattern bank has an AUC of 0.50 and flags
nothing there. The model fused from the three original signals is the only one
that keeps recall (0.54--0.61) with few false alarms (0.00--0.12) on BIPIA, and on
PoisonedRAG it flags 0.48--0.68 of the clean documents.

Two repairs help only in part. Setting the threshold from 60 clean documents of
the new source brings the false-alarm rate back to 0.044--0.051, but not recall:
the sentence classifier reaches 0.05--0.08 on BIPIA and 0.05--0.11 on
PoisonedRAG. Adding 40 labelled attacks from the new source to the training data
lifts the fused model to 0.53--0.59 on BIPIA and 0.19--0.35 on PoisonedRAG, with
false-alarm rates up to 0.09. A threshold set on pooled sources therefore
describes those sources, and a new source needs its own calibration and, for
recall, its own labelled attacks.

\subsection{What the strongest signal learned}
\label{sec:learned}
Most of the held-out recall comes from the sentence classifier, so we asked
whether it detects instructions or the style of each corpus. We fitted a control
that sees only character $n$-grams (no word-level instruction cues) on the same
training role and set its threshold the same way. The decision rule was fixed
before running: the classifier's recall on PoisonedRAG is called consistent with
generator style if the control reaches at least 80\% of it,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\ \ge\ 0.8\cdot\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{clf}} .
    \label{eq:stylerule}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:style} shows that the rule fires in all three seeds. The
style-only control recovers more of the PoisonedRAG passages than the classifier
does (0.79 to 0.93 against 0.43 to 0.47), and the two have almost the same
document AUC there (0.94 to 0.97 against 0.95 to 0.96). The control overshoots the
5\% budget in two seeds, but not in seed 43, where it still reaches 0.79. The
most heavily weighted features point the same way. In the training role of seed
42, ``renowned'' occurs in 30 poisoned and no clean documents, ``surprisingly''
in 22 and none, and ``basketball'' in 13 and none, all of them PoisonedRAG
passages: topic prose, not instructions. On BIPIA the picture is the opposite
(classifier 0.82 to 0.91, control 0.24 to 0.28) and the top features are
imperatives and code tokens, which fits instruction detection, but only within
that corpus. We therefore do not credit the PoisonedRAG recall to instruction
detection: part of the strongest signal is a corpus artefact, and it should not
be expected to carry over to a new generator.

\subsection{Development-set case studies}
\label{sec:devset}
Before the pooled protocol, we studied the three original signals on 77 documents:
55 clean READMEs of ten open-source projects and 60 poisoned documents (15 per
category: direct injection, fabricated system-authority messages, on-topic
``corpus poisoning'' with harmful advice and no trigger phrases, and garbled
text). The embedding reference corpus and its evaluation set were disjoint (38
trusted clean documents; the other 17 clean and all 60 poisoned documents were
scored). Thresholds and calibration bounds were chosen on these same 77
documents, so every number in this subsection is optimistic and none of it is a
held-out result (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). We keep it because the failure
cases it exposed shaped the later design.

\paragraph{Score range and calibration.}
Let $s_i\in[0,1]$ be the score of detector $i$, and let $[\ell_i,h_i]$ be the
range its scores occupy. The naive fusion is the plain average, and the
calibrated fusion first rescales each score to $[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{naive}}=\frac13\sum_{i=1}^{3}s_i,\qquad
    \hat s_i=\frac{s_i-\ell_i}{h_i-\ell_i},\qquad
    s_{\text{cal}}=\frac13\sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat s_i .
    \label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
Since $s_i=\ell_i+r_i\hat s_i$ with $r_i=h_i-\ell_i$, the naive average is an
exact reweighting of the calibrated scores,
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{naive}}=\frac13\sum_{i}\ell_i+\sum_{i}\frac{r_i}{3}\,\hat s_i ,
    \label{eq:reweight}
\end{equation}
so a detector with a narrow score range counts for less however discriminative it
is. The embedding detector's raw scores occupied $[0.180,0.430]$, while pattern
and perplexity spanned $[0,1]$. Table~\ref{tab:fusion} shows the effect: calibrating
before averaging raised F1 from 0.79 to 0.97 with no change to any detector, and a
learned fusion on raw scores (F1 0.88) gave the embedding detector the smallest
coefficient (1.09, against 1.82 and 1.51) although a per-detector threshold sweep
shows it is the strongest signal (F1 0.92 alone at its best threshold, against
0.55 and 0.49 for pattern and perplexity). On calibrated inputs the same model
reaches F1 0.98 and ranks the embedding detector first (coefficient 2.67).

\paragraph{Failure map by attack category.}
Per category, the fraction of poisoned documents caught at each detector's own
best threshold was: injection 67\% (pattern), 73\% (embedding), 33\% (perplexity),
100\% (calibrated fusion); authority 73, 93, 0 and 93\%; corpus poisoning 13, 100,
0 and 93\%; garbled text 0, 100, 100 and 100\%. No single detector covers every
category. Corpus poisoning, harmful advice in ordinary technical language, was
caught almost entirely by the embedding detector, but part of that success is
likely topic distance from the README baseline rather than detection of malicious
intent (see below).

\paragraph{A regex gap found by failure analysis.}
Inspection of the missed documents found two injections containing ``ignore
\textit{your} previous instructions,'' which the original expression did not
match because it allowed only one optional modifier before ``instructions.''
Accepting a possessive pronoun raised injection-category detection from 87\% to
100\% and calibrated fusion F1 from 0.95 to 0.97, with no new false positives
on the full corpus.

\paragraph{Adversarial red-teaming.}
We wrote ten documents meant to defeat all three detectors: no trigger phrases,
fluent text, and README vocabulary to keep embedding distance small. Nine of ten
were caught in the first round. The evasion, \texttt{adv\_010}, opened with ``this
project is distributed under the MIT license'' and then described build artifacts
published with default demo credentials. We call this \emph{boilerplate
anchoring}: prefixing a malicious clause with text so common in the reference
domain that it pulls the whole-document embedding toward the baseline and dilutes
the payload (combined score 0.106 against a threshold of 0.15). We then added two
credential-exposure patterns, which match three of the ten adversarial documents
and none of the 55 clean or 12 cross-domain documents, and all ten are now
caught. Because the patterns were written after reading \texttt{adv\_010}, this is
a fix for a found gap, \textbf{not} independent evidence of robustness.

\paragraph{Calibration fragility.}\label{sec:calibration-fragility}
Min-max bounds are set by one document at each extreme. Removing the single
document that sets the embedding detector's lower bound moves it from 0.180 to
0.205 and shifts the normalised embedding scores by 2.1\% on average, about 1.8
times more than under 5th/95th-percentile bounds (1.1\%). Quantile bounds are not
automatically better: \texttt{adv\_010} receives a lower embedding contribution
under them (0.215) than under min-max (0.318).

\paragraph{Cross-domain false positives.}
Twelve benign documents (HR, support and office policies) were scored with the
same baseline and threshold, and all 12 were flagged, each driven mainly by the
embedding detector. Their median embedding score (0.37) is essentially that of
the poisoned documents (0.36) and well above that of in-domain clean documents
(0.27). The baseline contains only software READMEs, so any document in another
style looks anomalous even when harmless: the embedding detector partly measures
distance from the baseline domain, not malicious intent.

\paragraph{Negative result: segment-level scoring.}
To address boilerplate anchoring we scored each sentence independently with three
aggregations (maximum, a blend of maximum and mean, and a high-threshold veto).
None helped: maximum aggregation raised clean false positives from 4 to 9 of 17
at the original threshold, and after re-tuning the peak F1 values were 0.90, 0.90
and 0.92, none above the 0.92 of the unmodified document-level detector. The
highest segment score of \texttt{adv\_010} (0.319) was below the maximum segment
score of 5 of 17 clean documents.
